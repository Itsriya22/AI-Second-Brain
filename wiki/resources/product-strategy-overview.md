# Product Strategy Overview

A comprehensive guide covering market classification, strategic planning frameworks, and AI impact on product strategy.

Product Management: Markets and
Strategic Planning
Market Deﬁnition and Classiﬁcation
Core Deﬁnition
A market is a speciﬁc group of potential customers who have a particular need that a product aims to
address. Every business requires both a product and a market to exist.
Market Classiﬁcation Framework
1. Consumer Markets
Mass Markets
Broad demographic appeal with cross-segment usage
Examples: Social media platforms, streaming services
Characteristics: High accessibility, wide reach
Niche Markets
Specialized segments with speciﬁc needs
Often command premium pricing
Examples: Electric vehicles, mental health applications
Smaller addressable market but less competition
Demographic Segmentation
Age-based: Gen Z, Millennials, Gen X, retirees
Life stage: New parents, students, working professionals
Income level: Budget-conscious vs. premium segments
Gender-speciﬁc products and services
2. Enterprise Markets
Small and Medium Enterprises (SMEs)

Limited resources, simpler requirements
Price-sensitive with basic functionality needs
Examples: Basic CRM tools, simple e-commerce platforms
Mid-Market
Growing companies with moderate complexity
Balance between functionality and cost
Often require scalable solutions
Large Enterprises
Complex requirements, higher budgets
Longer sales cycles, extensive decision processes
Need comprehensive, integrated solutions
3. Vertical Markets (Industry-Speciﬁc)
Healthcare
Telemedicine platforms, diagnostic tools
Regulatory compliance requirements
Education
Learning management systems, assessment tools
Varied stakeholder needs (students, teachers, institutions)
Retail
Inventory management, customer analytics
Omnichannel integration requirements
4. Geographic Markets
Urban vs. Tier 2/3 Cities
Diﬀerent infrastructure capabilities
Varying price sensitivity and digital adoption
Distinct behavioral patterns
Global North vs. Global South

Development level diﬀerences
Infrastructure and economic disparities
Behavioral and cultural variations
5. Psychographic Markets
Health-Conscious Segment
Prioritize wellness and ﬁtness
Willing to pay premium for health beneﬁts
Status-Seekers
Purchase decisions driven by social positioning
Value exclusivity and brand prestige
Eco-Conscious Users
Environmental sustainability priority
Prefer sustainable and ethical products
Technology Adoption Patterns
Tech optimists: Early adopters, experimental
Tech skeptics: Cautious, need proof of value
Critical for new technology launches
DIY Enthusiasts
Prefer self-service and customization
Cultural variations (more common in Western markets)
Important for no-code and self-service tools
AI Impact on Products and Markets
AI Disrupting Products
Enhanced product intelligence and automation
Multi-step task capabilities (agentic products)
Voice and vision interfaces
Accelerated development and prototyping

Hyper-personalized user experiences
Continuous learning from user interactions
AI Disrupting Markets
Creation of entirely new market categories
Disintermediation of traditional intermediaries
Faster emergence of micro-markets and niches
Lower barriers to market entry
Dynamic pricing and personalization at scale
Global scalability with minimal marginal costs
Strategic Planning Framework
Corporate Planning Hierarchy


Two-Cycle Product Management Framework
Key Principle: New products follow Cycle 1 then join Cycle 2. New features for existing products
also require Cycle 1 processes.
Organizational Structures
Functional Structure (Most Common)
Product managers have responsibility without direct authority
Must collaborate across separate Engineering, Marketing, and Sales teams
Requires strong coordination and inﬂuence skills
Product-Centric Structure (Rare)
All functions report directly to product manager
Primarily found in startups
Diﬃcult to scale in larger organizations

Product Lifecycle Management
Lifecycle Stages
Modern Lifecycle Reality
Product lifecycles have shortened from 10+ years to maximum 1 year
Digital products require constant updates and iterations
Continuous innovation essential for lifecycle extension
Strategic Analysis Frameworks
SWOT Analysis
Most fundamental strategic analysis tool
Strengths: Internal positive factors and capabilities
Weaknesses: Internal limitations and disadvantages
Opportunities: External positive possibilities for growth
Threats: External negative factors that could impact business
Application: Can be used for products, companies, or business units. Essential for regular strategic
assessment.
Example - TechEdu

BCG Growth Matrix
Maps products based on:
Market Growth Rate (vertical axis)
Relative Market Share (horizontal axis)
Four Categories:
Star: High growth, high share - invest heavily
Cash Cow: Low growth, high share - generate cash
Question Mark: High growth, low share - invest or divest decision
Dog: Low growth, low share - consider divestment
Current Relevance: Limited practical application due to static nature. Better for corporate-level
portfolio decisions.

GE-McKinsey Portfolio Matrix
Nine-box matrix based on:
Industry Attractiveness (vertical axis)
Competitive Strength (horizontal axis)
Strategic Actions:
Invest: High attractiveness + High strength
Protect: Medium combinations
Harvest: Declining positions
Divest: Low attractiveness + Low strength
GE McKinsey Matrix Explained With Step-By-Step Guide
Limitations: Highly subjective measurements, complex implementation.
Ansoﬀ Matrix (Critical Framework)
Most practical tool for product managers
Based on two dimensions:
Products: Existing vs. New
Markets: Existing vs. New
Four Strategic Options:
Market Penetration (Existing Product + Existing Market)

Lowest risk strategy
Focus: Increase market share with current oﬀerings
Tactics: Enhanced marketing, deeper market penetration
Challenges: Market saturation, intense competition
Product Development (New Product + Existing Market)
Medium risk level
Focus: Leverage existing market knowledge with new oﬀerings
Beneﬁts: Established customer relationships and distribution
Challenges: Product development costs, competitive responses
Market Development (Existing Product + New Market)
Medium risk level
Focus: Geographic or demographic expansion
Beneﬁts: Proven product capabilities
Challenges: No brand recognition, market education requirements
Diversiﬁcation (New Product + New Market)
Highest risk strategy
Focus: Complete strategic pivot
Beneﬁts: Unlimited growth potential
Challenges: High uncertainty, signiﬁcant resource requirements
Resource Allocation Strategy
Most organizations use portfolio approach:
Market Penetration: 40-50% (lowest risk)
Product Development: 25-30%
Market Development: 20-25%
Diversiﬁcation: 5-10% (highest risk)
Case Study: TechEdu Strategic Analysis
Company Proﬁle
EdTech company founded 2012
Provides short-term online courses in technology, project management, and analytics

Target market: Students preparing for IT company recruitment
Revenue model: 90% B2C (students), 10% B2B (corporate training)
Current challenge: Revenue stagnation after 10 years of growth
Market Context Changes
Reduced mass recruitment by IT companies
Companies developing in-house training capabilities
Students questioning value of paying for training companies will provide
Technology course content becoming obsolete with AI advancement
SWOT Analysis Results
Strengths:
Established brand recognition (10+ years)
Extensive content library across three domains
Maintained proﬁtability despite challenges
Aﬀordable pricing for target segment
Weaknesses:
Outdated technology course content
Over-dependence on B2C market (90%)
Underutilized B2B potential
No new course categories in years
Opportunities:
B2B market expansion
Emerging technology courses (AI/ML)
College partnerships
Mid-career professional market
International expansion
Threats:
Declining IT mass recruitment
Increased EdTech competition
Self-suﬃcient college programs
Corporate in-house training growth

Product Lifecycle Assessment
Current position: Transition from Maturity to Decline
Indicators: Flattening revenue, market saturation, increased competition
Strategic imperative: Innovation or market expansion required

Modiﬁed Ansoﬀ Matrix
Market Penetration:
Enhanced digital marketing
College tie-up programs
Social media presence expansion
Product Development:
AI/ML certiﬁcation programs
Career coaching services
Soft skills training modules

Mid-career professionals segment
Geographic expansion (MENA region)
Enhanced corporate market focus

Industry-speciﬁc training (healthcare, ﬁnance)

Strategic Recommendations
1. Update and modernize technology course oﬀerings
2. Develop B2B sales and delivery capabilities
3. Target mid-career upskilling market
4. Establish strategic college partnerships
5. Launch micro-certiﬁcation programs
Key Strategic Principles
Framework Selection
SWOT: Foundation for all strategic analysis
Ansoﬀ Matrix: Most practical for product management decisions
BCG/GE-McKinsey: Useful for portfolio-level corporate decisions
Two-Cycle Framework: Overall structure for product management process
Portfolio Management
Diversify risk across multiple strategic approaches
Allocate majority resources to lower-risk strategies
Regular strategic assessment (minimum annually)
Balance existing product optimization with new product development
Modern Considerations
AI disruption aﬀecting both products and markets simultaneously
Shortened product lifecycles requiring constant innovation
Psychographic segmentation increasingly important
Global scalability opportunities through technology platforms
